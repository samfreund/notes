## Kinematics Solutions

### Question 1 (Conceptual)

The displacement $s$ for an object starting from rest ($v_0 = 0$) and accelerating uniformly is given by the kinematic equation:
$$s = v_0 t + \frac{1}{2} a t^2 \implies s = \frac{1}{2} a t^2$$

**Calculate the distance for Car A:**
*   Acceleration ($a_A$): $2\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$
*   Time ($t_A$): $10\,\mathrm{s}$
$$s_A = \frac{1}{2} (2\,\mathrm{m/s^2}) (10\,\mathrm{s})^2$$
$$s_A = (1\,\mathrm{m/s^2}) (100\,\mathrm{s^2})$$
$$s_A = 100 m$$

**Calculate the distance for Car B:**
*   Acceleration ($a_B$): $4\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$
*   Time ($t_B$): $5\,\mathrm{s}$
$$s_B = \frac{1}{2} (4\,\mathrm{m/s^2}) (5\,\mathrm{s})^2$$
$$s_B = (2\,\mathrm{m/s^2}) (25\,\mathrm{s^2})$$
$$s_B = 50m$$

**Comparison:** Since $s_A = 100\,\mathrm{m}$ and $s_B = 50\,\mathrm{m}$, **Car A** travels farther. The distance traveled is proportional to the acceleration ($a$) and the square of the time ($t^2$). In this case, the longer time Car A spends accelerating has a greater impact on the final distance than the higher acceleration of Car B over a shorter time.

### Question 2 (Calculation)

**Given values:**
*   Initial velocity ($v_0$): $0\,\mathrm{m/s}$ (starts from rest)
*   Final velocity ($v$): $20\,\mathrm{m/s}$
*   Distance ($s$): $200\,\mathrm{m}$

#### (a) Identify the appropriate kinematic equation.

To find the acceleration without knowing the time, the best equation is:
$$v^2 = v_0^2 + 2 a s$$

#### (b) Calculate the cyclist’s acceleration.

Rearrange the equation to solve for acceleration ($a$):
$a = \frac{v^2 - v_0^2}{2s}$

Substitute the known values:
$$a = \frac{(20\,\mathrm{m/s})^2 - (0\,\mathrm{m/s})^2}{2 (200\,\mathrm{m})}$$
$$a = \frac{400\,\mathrm{m^2/s^2}}{400\,\mathrm{m}}$$

The acceleration is:
$a = 1.0\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$

#### (c) Determine the time taken to reach 20 m/s.

Use the equation that relates initial velocity, final velocity, acceleration, and time:
$$v = v_0 + a t$$

Rearrange to solve for time ($t$):
$t = \frac{v - v_0}{a}$

Substitute the values:
$$t = \frac{20\,\mathrm{m/s} - 0\,\mathrm{m/s}}{1.0\,\mathrm{m/s^2}}$$
$t = 20\,\mathrm{s}$

## Waves and Thermodynamics Solutions

### Question 1 (Conceptual)

The speed of sound in an ideal gas, like air, is directly related to the absolute temperature. The relationship is approximately given by:
$$v \propto \sqrt{T}$$
where $T$ is the absolute temperature (in Kelvin).

Since 30 °C is a higher temperature than 0 °C, the sound waves propagating through the $30\,\mathrm{^\circ C}$ air will travel **faster**. This is because the molecules in the warmer air are moving faster and can therefore transmit the sound energy (the mechanical vibration) more quickly.

### Question 2 (Calculation)

**Given values:**
*   Speed of sound in helium at $20\,\mathrm{^\circ C}$ ($v_1$): $1,007\,\mathrm{m/s}$
*   Distance ($d$): $2.5\,\mathrm{km} = 2,500\,\mathrm{m}$

#### (a) Write down the equation relating speed, distance, and time.

The basic relationship for constant speed is:
$v = \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{t}}$

#### (b) Calculate the travel time of the pulse.

Rearrange the equation to solve for time ($t$):
$t = \frac{d}{v}$

Substitute the known values:
$$t_1 = \frac{2,500\,\mathrm{m}}{1,007\,\mathrm{m/s}}$$

The travel time is:
$t_1 \approx 2.48\,\mathrm{s}$

#### (c) If the temperature increases to 100 °C and the speed of sound in helium increases by 10%, what is the new travel time over the same distance?

First, calculate the new speed ($v_2$):
$$v_2 = v_1 + 0.10 \cdot v_1 = 1.10 \cdot v_1$$
$v_2 = 1.10 \cdot 1,007\,\mathrm{m/s}$

The new speed is:
$v_2 = 1,107.7\,\mathrm{m/s}$

Now, calculate the new travel time ($t_2$) over $d = 2,500\,\mathrm{m}$:
$$t_2 = \frac{d}{v_2}$$
$$t_2 = \frac{2,500\,\mathrm{m}}{1,107.7\,\mathrm{m/s}}$$

The new travel time is:
$t_2 \approx 2.26\,\mathrm{s}$

## Conservation of Energy Solutions

### Question 1 (Conceptual)

The work–energy theorem, in the context of conservative forces (like gravity), states that the total mechanical energy of a system is conserved if no non-conservative forces (like friction or air resistance) do work on the system.

**Total Mechanical Energy ($E$):**
$$E = \text{Kinetic Energy} (K) + \text{Potential Energy} (U_g)$$
$$E = \frac{1}{2} m v^2 + m g h$$

Since friction is neglected, gravity is the only force doing work. Gravity is a conservative force.

**Comparison:** The roller coaster car's total mechanical energy **remains the same** (is conserved) at both the top of the track and the point 30 m below. At the top, the energy is purely potential. As the car moves down, the potential energy ($U_g$) decreases, and the kinetic energy ($K$) increases, but their sum ($E$) stays constant.

### Question 2 (Calculation)

**Given values:**
*   Mass of the block ($m$): $2.0\,\mathrm{kg}$
*   Initial height ($h$): $10\,\mathrm{m}$
*   Length of the incline ($L$): $20\,\mathrm{m}$
*   Gravitational acceleration ($g$): $9.8\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$

#### (a) Identify the equation for conservation of mechanical energy.

Since the incline is initially frictionless, the total mechanical energy is conserved:
$$E_{initial} = E_{final}$$
$$K_i + U_{g,i} = K_f + U_{g,f}$$
$$\frac{1}{2} m v_i^2 + m g h_i = \frac{1}{2} m v_f^2 + m g h_f$$

#### (b) Calculate the block’s speed at the bottom.

*   Initial speed ($v_i$): $0\,\mathrm{m/s}$ (released from rest)
*   Final height ($h_f$): $0\,\mathrm{m}$ (at the bottom)
*   Initial height ($h_i$): $10\,\mathrm{m}$

The conservation equation simplifies to:
$$0 + m g h_i = \frac{1}{2} m v_f^2 + 0$$

The mass ($m$) cancels out:
$$g h_i = \frac{1}{2} v_f^2$$

Solve for the final speed ($v_f$):
$v_f = \sqrt{2 g h_i}$

Substitute the values:
$$v_f = \sqrt{2 (9.8\,\mathrm{m/s^2}) (10\,\mathrm{m})}$$
$$v_f = \sqrt{196\,\mathrm{m^2/s^2}}$$

$$v_f = 14\,\mathrm{m/s}$$

#### (c) If a constant friction force of 15 N acts along the incline, how much mechanical energy is lost to heat?

The energy lost is equal to the work done by the non-conservative friction force ($W_{nc}$), which is negative.
$$W_{nc} = \vec{F}_{friction} \cdot \vec{d}$$
The friction force ($F_f$) is $15\,\mathrm{N}$ and acts opposite to the displacement ($L$). The displacement is $20\,\mathrm{m}$.
$$W_{friction} = -F_f L$$
$$W_{friction} = -(15\,\mathrm{N}) (20\,\mathrm{m})$$
$W_{friction} = -300\,\mathrm{J}$

The amount of mechanical energy lost to heat is the magnitude of the work done by friction: $300\,\mathrm{J}$.

#### (d) What is the block’s speed at the bottom with friction?

The full work–energy theorem states that the work done by non-conservative forces equals the change in total mechanical energy ($\Delta E$):
$$W_{friction} = \Delta E = E_{final} - E_{initial}$$
$$W_{friction} = (\frac{1}{2} m v_{f}^2 + m g h_f) - (\frac{1}{2} m v_{i}^2 + m g h_i)$$

Substitute $v_i = 0$ and $h_f = 0$:
$$W_{friction} = \frac{1}{2} m v_{f}^2 - m g h_i$$

Rearrange to solve for $v_f$:
$$\frac{1}{2} m v_{f}^2 = m g h_i + W_{friction}$$
$$v_{f}^2 = \frac{2}{m} (m g h_i + W_{friction})$$
$$v_f = \sqrt{\frac{2}{m} (m g h_i + W_{friction})}$$

We know: $m g h_i = (2.0\,\mathrm{kg})(9.8\,\mathrm{m/s^2})(10\,\mathrm{m}) = 196\,\mathrm{J}$.
$$v_f = \sqrt{\frac{2}{2.0\,\mathrm{kg}} (196\,\mathrm{J} - 300\,\mathrm{J})}$$
We find the work of friction is $W_{friction} = -300\,\mathrm{J}$. Since the total initial energy is only $196\,\mathrm{J}$, the friction work of $300\,\mathrm{J}$ is greater than the total available energy.

Thus, the block can only travel a distance $\Delta L$ where the work of friction equals the total initial potential energy, $mgh$.
$$F_f \Delta L = m g h$$
$\Delta L = \frac{196\,\mathrm{J}}{15\,\mathrm{N}} \approx 13.07\,\mathrm{m}$

Since the incline is $20\,\mathrm{m}$ long, the block stops prior to reaching the bottom. The total initial energy is $196\,\mathrm{J}$. The energy lost is $300\,\mathrm{J}$. The block comes to a stop when its kinetic energy is zero, which means its final speed is $0\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

As the block does not reach the end of the incline, it's speed at the end of the incline does not exist.

## Rotation Solutions

### Question 1 (Conceptual)

The rotational kinetic energy ($K_{rot}$) is given by the equation:
$K_{rot} = \frac{1}{2} I \omega^2$
where $I$ is the moment of inertia and $\omega$ is the angular speed. Since both discs have the same angular speed ($\omega$), the one with the greater moment of inertia ($I$) will have the greater rotational kinetic energy.

For a uniform disc, the moment of inertia about its center is:
$$I = \frac{1}{2} M R^2$$

Both discs have identical mass ($M$), but $R_Y = 1.0\,\mathrm{m}$ and $R_X = 0.5\,\mathrm{m}$.
The moment of inertia is proportional to the square of the radius ($R^2$). Since $R_Y$ is twice $R_X$, $I_Y$ is four times $I_X$:
$$I_Y \propto (1.0\,\mathrm{m})^2 = 1.0$$
$$I_X \propto (0.5\,\mathrm{m})^2 = 0.25$$

Since $I_Y > I_X$, **Disc Y** has the greater rotational kinetic energy. This shows that mass distributed farther from the axis of rotation significantly increases the moment of inertia, and thus the rotational energy.

### Question 2 (Calculation)

**Given values:**
*   Mass of the sphere ($M$): $3.0\,\mathrm{kg}$
*   Radius of the sphere ($R$): $0.2\,\mathrm{m}$
*   Incline length ($L$): $5.0\,\mathrm{m}$
*   Incline angle ($\theta$): $30^\circ$

#### (a) Write the relationship between translational and rotational kinetic energy for rolling without slipping.

For rolling without slipping, the total kinetic energy ($K_{total}$) is the sum of the translational kinetic energy ($K_{trans}$) and the rotational kinetic energy ($K_{rot}$):
$$K_{total} = K_{trans} + K_{rot}$$
$$K_{total} = \frac{1}{2} M v^2 + \frac{1}{2} I \omega^2$$
And for rolling without slipping, the relationship between linear speed ($v$) and angular speed ($\omega$) is:
$\omega = \frac{v}{R}$

#### (b) Calculate the sphere’s speed at the bottom.

Use the conservation of energy principle, where the initial potential energy ($U_g$) is converted to total kinetic energy ($K_{total}$).
$U_{g,i} = K_{total,f}$

First, find the height ($h$) the sphere drops:
$$h = L \sin(\theta) = (5.0\,\mathrm{m}) \sin(30^\circ) = (5.0\,\mathrm{m}) (0.5)$$
$h = 2.5\,\mathrm{m}$

The moment of inertia for a solid sphere is $I = \frac{2}{5} M R^2$.

Substitute $I$ and $\omega$ into the energy conservation equation:
$$M g h = \frac{1}{2} M v^2 + \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{2}{5} M R^2 \right) \left( \frac{v}{R} \right)^2$$

Simplify the rotational term:
$$\frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{2}{5} M R^2 \right) \left( \frac{v^2}{R^2} \right) = \frac{1}{5} M v^2$$

The conservation equation becomes (the mass $M$ cancels out):
$$M g h = \frac{1}{2} M v^2 + \frac{1}{5} M v^2$$
$$g h = v^2 \left( \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{5} \right)$$
$$g h = v^2 \left( \frac{5+2}{10} \right)$$
$$g h = \frac{7}{10} v^2$$

Solve for the final speed ($v$):
$$v = \sqrt{\frac{10}{7} g h}$$

Substitute the values $g = 9.8\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ and $h = 2.5\,\mathrm{m}$:
$$v = \sqrt{\frac{10}{7} (9.8\,\mathrm{m/s^2}) (2.5\,\mathrm{m})}$$
$$v = \sqrt{\frac{10}{7} (24.5\,\mathrm{m^2/s^2})}$$

The speed at the bottom is:
$v \approx 5.92\,\mathrm{m/s}$

#### (c) Determine the total rotational kinetic energy at the bottom.

First, calculate the moment of inertia ($I$):
$$I = \frac{2}{5} M R^2 = \frac{2}{5} (3.0\,\mathrm{kg}) (0.2\,\mathrm{m})^2$$
$$I = \frac{2}{5} (3.0\,\mathrm{kg}) (0.04\,\mathrm{m^2})$$
$$I = \frac{0.24}{5}\,\mathrm{kg \cdot m^2}$$
$I = 0.048\,\mathrm{kg \cdot m^2}$

Next, calculate the angular speed ($\omega$):
$$\omega = \frac{v}{R} = \frac{5.916\,\mathrm{m/s}}{0.2\,\mathrm{m}}$$
